Q.01
The Volatile Paradox
MEMORY FLASH vs EEPROM HARD
A microcontroller is running a data-logging program. It reads sensor values into RAM every 100ms and periodically writes summaries to Flash. During a test, the power is cut abruptly. The engineer discovers that the last 47 sensor readings are lost, but all previous summaries are intact — except the most recent one, which is corrupted rather than simply missing.
(a) Explain precisely why the last 47 readings vanished, invoking the physical nature of RAM at the electron level of abstraction available in this course.
(b) Why might the most recent Flash summary be corrupted rather than simply absent? What does this reveal about the nature of Flash's block-wise erasure mechanism?
(c) If EEPROM were used instead of Flash for summaries, would corruption still be possible? Justify using the byte-wise vs block-wise erasure distinction.
Complete Solution
Part (a) — Why RAM data vanished
RAM (Random Access Memory) is volatile: it stores data by maintaining electrical charge states in capacitors (DRAM) or by transistor latch states (SRAM). These states require continuous power to sustain. When power is cut, capacitors discharge and transistor flip-flops lose their bistable configuration. There is no chemical or structural change — just the collapse of an active electrical state. The 47 readings existed as voltage differences on microscopic capacitors; with no power, those voltages equalized to zero. The data was never "written" anywhere permanent.
Key insight: RAM stores 1s and 0s as "is there charge here?" — not as any physical change to the material. It is fundamentally ephemeral.
Part (b) — Why Flash was corrupted, not simply absent
Flash memory erases in blocks (large chunks of data). A write to Flash involves two steps: (1) erase the entire block, then (2) write the new data. If power is cut between these two steps — after the block was erased but before the new data was fully written — the result is a block that has been cleared but not refilled. Some cells may be in partially programmed states, yielding garbage data. This explains corruption rather than absence: the old data was destroyed by the erase phase, and the new data was never fully committed.
Block-wise erasure is atomic at the block level but NOT atomic at the write level. The window between erase and write is a vulnerability zone.

This is why critical embedded systems use "wear-levelling" and "journaling" techniques — they ensure a backup copy always exists before the old one is erased, making writes truly atomic from the system's perspective.

Part (c) — EEPROM vs Flash: Is corruption still possible?
EEPROM is byte-wise erasable — individual bytes can be erased and rewritten independently. This dramatically reduces the corruption window. To update a single byte, you only erase and rewrite that one byte, not an entire block. However, corruption is still theoretically possible: if power is cut mid-byte-write, that single byte may be in an indeterminate state. The advantage is that corruption is localised to one byte rather than potentially an entire block of data. For small configuration data (as the lecture notes describe), this makes EEPROM significantly more robust for frequent, small updates.
EEPROM doesn't eliminate corruption risk — it minimises its blast radius. One corrupted byte is catastrophically better than one corrupted block.
Q.02
The Clock Stretching Trap
I2C PROTOCOL HARD
In an I2C network, a master device attempts to send data to a slow slave sensor at 400 kHz (Fast Mode). The slave repeatedly invokes clock stretching, holding SCL low for extended periods.
(a) From first principles of the I2C protocol, explain how a slave device — which has no authority to generate the clock — can nevertheless control its timing. What physical mechanism allows this?
(b) A student argues: "If the slave holds SCL low indefinitely, the master is permanently blocked. This is a fundamental protocol flaw." Construct a rigorous defence of why this is actually a feature, not a bug, citing what would happen alternatively if the master simply continued clocking.
(c) Now consider: a second master on the same bus also wishes to transmit simultaneously. Walk through the collision detection and arbitration process step by step, identifying the exact moment and mechanism by which one master "wins."
Complete Solution
Part (a) — Physical mechanism of clock stretching
I2C uses an open-drain (wired-AND) bus topology. Both SCL and SDA lines are pulled HIGH by resistors (Rp), but any device on the bus can pull them LOW by activating its open-drain transistor. The line is HIGH only when no device is pulling it low. A slave cannot generate a rising edge (it cannot push the line high), but it CAN hold SCL low by keeping its transistor active. The master checks that SCL actually went high before proceeding to the next clock phase — if it finds SCL still low (held by the slave), it simply waits. The slave controls timing not by generating a clock, but by vetoing the release of the clock line.
Open-drain = you can only pull down, never push up. Control through suppression, not assertion.
Part (b) — Defence of clock stretching as a feature
If the master continued clocking regardless, it would shift new bits onto SDA while the slave has not yet read the current bit. The slave's internal buffer would overflow — it would receive garbled or misaligned data. The master would believe transmission succeeded while the slave received nothing useful. This would be a silent failure — far worse than a temporary stall. Clock stretching ensures the I2C bus is self-throttling: the slowest component in the transaction sets the pace. The system degrades gracefully to the capability of the weakest device, rather than catastrophically mis-transmitting data.

Clock stretching is essentially a hardware-level "flow control" mechanism — analogous to TCP's window size in networking. The slow receiver signals "wait" rather than dropping packets silently.

Part (c) — Multi-master arbitration
Both masters detect the bus is idle (SDA and SCL high) and simultaneously issue a START condition. Both begin transmitting their destination addresses bit by bit. During transmission, each master simultaneously monitors the SDA line. Because of the wired-AND property: if Master A writes a '1' (releases SDA) but Master B writes a '0' (pulls SDA low), the bus reads '0'. Master A observes: "I sent a 1, but the bus is 0 — someone else is here." Master A immediately stops transmitting and becomes a slave-receiver. Master B, whose transmitted bit matches what it reads on the bus, notices no discrepancy and continues. Arbitration is lost the instant a master transmits HIGH but reads LOW. The master transmitting the lower address (more leading zeros) statistically wins. No data is corrupted because the winning master's bits are exactly what appear on the bus.
Arbitration is lossless — the "loser" simply defers, and the winning transmission completes correctly. Neither master needs to retry from scratch.
Q.03
The Baud Rate Boundary
UART PROTOCOL QUANTITATIVE
Two UART devices communicate. Device A transmits at exactly 9600 bps. Device B's oscillator runs at 9600 + Δ bps due to manufacturing tolerance.
(a) UART allows a maximum clock mismatch of ~10%. Derive from the packet structure (start bit, 8 data bits, parity, stop bit = 11 bits total) why the tolerance is approximately 10% and not some other value. Your derivation must reference the sampling mechanism.
(b) A packet is sent: 0b10110010 with even parity. Write out the complete bit sequence on the wire including all framing bits, with the correct parity bit calculated. Remember: LSB is transmitted first.
(c) The receiving UART samples each bit at the centre of its expected duration. If baud rate mismatch causes cumulative drift, at which bit position does the first sampling error most likely occur? Show your reasoning numerically.
Complete Solution
Part (a) — Deriving the ~10% tolerance
The receiver re-synchronises on the falling edge of the START bit (high-to-low transition). After that, it samples each subsequent bit at the midpoint of its expected duration. For a packet of 11 bits total, the last data bit (bit 10 after the start bit) is sampled at time: T_sample = 10.5 × T_bit (0.5 bit after the start edge for bit 1, plus 10 more bits).

For a sampling error to occur, the cumulative drift must shift the sample point by more than ±0.5 × T_bit (so it falls outside the correct bit window). With drift rate δ per bit, after 10.5 bits, total drift = 10.5δ. For safe sampling: 10.5δ < 0.5 T_bit, so δ < 1/21 ≈ 4.76% per bit. Over the full packet, total acceptable mismatch ≈ 1/10.5 ≈ 9.5% ≈ 10%.
The 10% figure is not arbitrary — it is geometrically derived from the ratio of half-a-bit-period to the number of bits until the last sample.
Part (b) — Complete bit sequence on wire
Data: 0b10110010 = binary 10110010.
LSB first transmission order: 0, 1, 0, 0, 1, 1, 0, 1 (bit0 to bit7).
Count of 1s in data: positions bit1, bit4, bit5, bit7 → four 1s total.
Even parity: total 1s must be even. Currently 4 ones → even. Parity bit = 0.
WIRE SEQUENCE (left = first transmitted):
[START=0] [0][1][0][0][1][1][0][1] [PARITY=0] [STOP=1]
Part (c) — First bit error location
With a 10% baud rate mismatch, drift accumulates at 0.1 T_bit per bit period. The receiver samples at the centre of each expected bit window. A sampling error occurs when cumulative drift exceeds 0.5 T_bit. After n bits: drift = 0.1n × T_bit. Setting 0.1n = 0.5 → n = 5. This means by the 5th bit after the start edge (the 5th data bit), drift has accumulated to half a bit period, placing the sample point at the boundary between two bits. The 5th data bit is the first position where missampling becomes likely. In practice, with 10% being the defined limit, errors begin to appear reliably at or after bit 5.

This is why engineering standards say "within 10%" rather than "exactly 10%" — there's no hard cliff, just a probability that rises as drift accumulates through the packet.

SECTION II Architecture & Classification
Q.04
The Von Neumann Bottleneck, Quantified
ARCHITECTURE HARVARD vs VON NEUMANN
A Harvard architecture MCU and a Von Neumann MCU both run at the same clock frequency f. The Von Neumann MCU requires 2 clock cycles per instruction; the Harvard MCU requires 1.
(a) An engineer claims the Harvard MCU is "twice as fast." Under what conditions is this claim exactly true, and under what realistic conditions does this advantage diminish or disappear?
(b) The Von Neumann bottleneck is described as limited throughput between CPU and memory. Explain why adding more RAM to a Von Neumann system does not solve the bottleneck, while widening the bus partially does.
(c) Modern systems use a "Modified Harvard Architecture" (as in the ATMega328P). What single modification reconciles the Harvard speed advantage with Von Neumann programming flexibility? What trade-off is introduced?
Complete Solution
Part (a) — When "twice as fast" is true, and when it isn't
The claim is exactly true when the workload is purely instruction-bound: every cycle is spent fetching and executing instructions, with no other delays. In reality, both architectures face the same peripheral I/O speeds, the same ADC conversion times, and the same external communication rates (UART, I2C, SPI). A program that spends 90% of its time waiting for a sensor reading gets negligible benefit from the 2× instruction speed on the remaining 10%. The advantage also shrinks with cache effects, branch mispredictions, and memory wait states that affect both architectures similarly.
Part (b) — Why more RAM doesn't fix the bottleneck
The bottleneck is the bandwidth of the shared bus — the rate at which bits can travel between memory and CPU per unit time. Adding more RAM increases capacity (how much can be stored) but not bandwidth (how fast data moves). The road is still one lane wide; you've just added more parking. Widening the bus (e.g., from 8-bit to 32-bit data bus) means 4× more data per clock cycle can transfer, directly addressing the bandwidth constraint. This is why bus width classification of MCUs is meaningful: it determines throughput, not storage.
RAM size = garage size. Bus width = number of lanes on the road. Congestion is a road problem, not a garage problem.
Part (c) — Modified Harvard Architecture
The modification is allowing the CPU to occasionally access program memory (Flash) as data — via special instructions like LPM (Load Program Memory) in AVR. Pure Harvard strictly separates the two buses; Modified Harvard adds a controlled pathway between them. This allows constants and lookup tables to be stored in Flash (saving precious RAM) without sacrificing the usual separation. The trade-off: this access path is typically slower and requires special instructions, so the programmer must explicitly manage which data goes where — it's not seamless like Von Neumann.

"Modified Harvard" is the architectural equivalent of having separate highways for trucks and cars, but a single on-ramp at one specific point. Mostly separated, occasionally merged — the best practical compromise.

Q.05
The Register Set Riddle
RISC vs CISC ARCHITECTURE HARD
(a) The RISC vs CISC table states RISC needs multiple register sets. This seems counterintuitive — more registers sounds more expensive. Explain why RISC needs multiple register sets by reasoning from what RISC does NOT have.
(b) A CISC processor executes MULTIPLY R1, [memory_address] in a single instruction. A RISC processor needs 3+ instructions for the same task. Yet the lecture data shows Apple's RISC chips outperforming Intel's CISC chips. Fully resolve this apparent contradiction.
(c) From the Intel vs Apple performance graph, Apple's trajectory has a steeper slope over time. Propose a physics/engineering reason rooted in transistor behaviour and chip design why RISC architectures scale better as transistors shrink.
Complete Solution
Part (a) — Why RISC needs multiple register sets
CISC has complex multi-step instructions that can implicitly save and restore register states (the hardware handles context). RISC instructions are simple and each instruction only does one thing. When a function call or interrupt occurs, the programmer/compiler must explicitly save all registers that might be overwritten. To make this efficient and avoid constant memory saves/restores, RISC uses multiple register windows or register banks — one set for the current function, another for a called function, allowing context switches without touching memory at all. The cost in silicon (more registers) is paid back in speed (no stack operations for function calls).
Part (b) — Resolving the RISC/CISC paradox
The confusion arises from conflating instructions per program with time per program. RISC needs more instructions, but each instruction executes in one clock cycle (fixed, simple format). CISC uses fewer instructions, but a single complex instruction may take 5–20 cycles. Furthermore: (1) RISC's simple, uniform instruction format makes pipelining trivial — you can overlap fetch/decode/execute stages cleanly. CISC's variable-length instructions make pipelining complex. (2) Modern compilers optimise RISC code extremely well. (3) RISC spends transistors on more execution units; CISC spends them on a complex decoder. More parallel execution units directly translates to throughput.
The performance metric is not instructions/second but work done per second. RISC wins by doing simpler things faster and more in parallel.
Part (c) — Why RISC scales better with transistor shrinkage
As transistors shrink, two physics effects dominate: (1) Power density increases — smaller transistors switching faster generate more heat per unit area. Complex CISC decoders are large, irregular logic blocks that are hard to power-gate and inefficient at smaller nodes. RISC's regular, repetitive structure (many identical execution units, uniform pipeline stages) allows better power gating, clock gating, and thermal management. (2) Wire delay becomes dominant — at small nodes, signals traveling across a large, irregular CISC decoder take longer relative to the gate delay. RISC's localised, regular structure keeps signal paths short. Additionally, the transistor budget freed from complex decoding can go into more cores or larger caches, both of which scale well.

Intel's CISC chips actually decode x86 instructions into micro-ops internally (which are RISC-like) — an acknowledgment that RISC principles win at execution. They just maintain the CISC instruction set for software compatibility.

Q.06
SPI vs I2C — The Wire Count Trade-off
SPI I2C QUANTITATIVE
An embedded system designer must connect 8 identical temperature sensors to a single microcontroller.
(a) Calculate the total number of wires required using I2C versus SPI. State all assumptions clearly.
(b) With I2C 7-bit addressing, 128 addresses are theoretically available. But the designer finds only 112 addresses usable in practice. What accounts for the missing 16?
(c) For SPI with 8 slaves, the designer proposes using a 3-to-8 decoder to reduce CS lines from 8 to 3. Can the master still address slaves individually? What limitation does this introduce?
(d) SPI is described as "full-duplex." Construct a concrete scenario where this full-duplex capability provides a measurable speed advantage over I2C's half-duplex nature.
Complete Solution
Part (a) — Wire count comparison
I2C: Requires only SDA + SCL = 2 wires total, regardless of number of slaves. All 8 sensors share these two lines. (Plus a common GND; but wires here means signal lines.)

SPI: Requires MOSI + MISO + SCLK + 1 CS per slave = 3 + 8 = 11 wires total for 8 slaves. Each slave needs its own dedicated Chip Select line for individual addressing.

SPIMOSI + MISO + SCLK + 8×CS11 wires
Part (b) — The missing 16 I2C addresses
The I2C specification reserves certain addresses for special purposes. Addresses 0000 000x (0x00) and 0000 001x through 0000 011x are reserved — including the General Call address (0x00, broadcasts to all slaves) and several startup/reserved addresses. Similarly, addresses 1111 xxx (0x78–0x7F) are reserved for 10-bit address extension mode. This gives: 8 reserved at the top (1111 0XX and 1111 1XX) + 8 reserved at the bottom (0000 0XX and 0000 1XX) = 16 reserved addresses, leaving 128 − 16 = 112 usable.
Part (c) — 3-to-8 decoder for CS lines
Yes, the master can still address all 8 slaves individually — the 3 input bits to the decoder select exactly one of 8 output lines. However, the critical limitation is: only one slave can be selected at a time, and selecting is sequential (the decoder must change state to switch slaves). With dedicated CS lines, the master could theoretically assert multiple CS lines simultaneously (for broadcast), or de-assert one while asserting another without propagation delay. With the decoder, there is always a transition period where no slave or a wrong slave is briefly selected. For systems requiring simultaneous multi-slave communication or very fast CS toggling, the decoder introduces glitch risk.
Part (d) — Full-duplex advantage scenario
Consider a SPI DAC (Digital-to-Analog Converter) with readback: the master sends a new output value to the DAC (via MOSI) while simultaneously reading back the DAC's previous output value for verification (via MISO) — all in one clock cycle sequence. In I2C (half-duplex), this would require two separate transactions: first a write, then a read, each with START/address/ACK overhead. SPI completes the write+readback in the time I2C spends just on the write. The speedup is approximately 2× for read-modify-write cycles on sensor/actuator pairs.

Full-duplex is most valuable not when you need raw speed, but when the read and write operations are logically coupled — you send a command and simultaneously receive the result of the previous command.

Q.07
The Parity Bit's Hidden Weakness
UART QUANTITATIVE HARD
(a) Demonstrate with a specific example that parity checking cannot detect a 2-bit error. What is the general rule for which error patterns evade parity detection?
(b) The parity bit adds 1 bit to every ~10-bit packet. Calculate the communication overhead percentage. What happens to effective data throughput if a system switches from 8-bit data frames to 5-bit data frames at the same baud rate? Is 5-bit mode more or less efficient?
(c) A more sophisticated system uses CRC (Cyclic Redundancy Check) instead of parity. Without knowing CRC details, reason from first principles: what property must a better error-detection scheme have that parity lacks? Express this as a mathematical/logical statement.
Complete Solution
Part (a) — Parity's blind spot
Send 10110010 — four 1s, even parity bit = 0. Now two bits flip: say bits 0 and 1 change: 01010010 — still four 1s (we swapped a 1 for 0 and a 0 for a 1), so parity is still even. The receiver calculates parity, finds it correct, and accepts the corrupted data with no error detected.

General rule: parity detects all odd numbers of bit errors (1, 3, 5…) and fails to detect all even numbers of bit errors (2, 4, 6…). This is because parity is a single XOR across all bits — flipping any even number of bits leaves the XOR sum unchanged.
Parity is a 1-bit checksum. Any error pattern with even Hamming weight is invisible to it.
Part (b) — Overhead calculation
8-bit frame: 1 start + 8 data + 1 parity + 1 stop = 11 bits total. Data bits = 8. Overhead = 3/11 = 27.3%. Efficiency = 8/11 = 72.7%.

5-bit frame: 1 start + 5 data + 1 parity + 1 stop = 8 bits total. Data bits = 5. Efficiency = 5/8 = 62.5%.

5-bit mode is less efficient — the fixed overhead (start + parity + stop = 3 bits) is amortized over fewer data bits, so it represents a larger fraction of total transmission time. This is a general principle: framing overhead has diminishing cost as data payload increases.
Larger data frames are more efficient — the same 3 bits of overhead carry more useful data. This is why modern protocols use large packet sizes.
Part (c) — What CRC has that parity lacks
Parity captures only one property of the data: whether the total number of 1s is odd or even. It maps the entire 8-bit space (256 values) to a single bit (0 or 1). This means 128 different data values share each parity bit — any error that maps between values in the same parity class is undetectable.

A better scheme needs: for any two distinct data patterns D₁ and D₂, the probability that their checksums are equal must be negligibly small (approaching 1/2ⁿ for an n-bit checksum). Formally: the checksum function C must have the property that C(D₁ XOR D₂) ≠ 0 for all common error patterns E = D₁ XOR D₂. CRC achieves this by treating data as polynomials and using polynomial division — errors that cause identical remainders are astronomically rare, not inevitable as with parity.

Parity asks "how many 1s?" CRC asks "what is the remainder when treated as a polynomial?" The latter encodes far more structural information about the data, making collisions (two different bit patterns with the same checksum) exponentially rarer.

SECTION IV Hardware vs Software, Interrupts & PWM
Q.08
The Interrupt Latency Problem
INTERRUPTS REAL-TIME HARD
A NodeMCU ESP8266 is running a motor control loop executing every 1ms (1 kHz). A hardware interrupt is triggered by an external sensor.
(a) The interrupt is described as "asynchronous." Explain precisely what this means in terms of the relationship between the interrupt signal and the CPU clock — and why this is fundamentally different from a software interrupt.
(b) The CPU cannot service an interrupt until the current instruction completes. For a RISC MCU (1-cycle instructions) vs a CISC MCU (1–20 cycle instructions), compare worst-case interrupt latencies. Which is better for real-time control, and why?
(c) A student writes an ISR that takes 800μs to execute, while the main motor loop runs every 1ms. Describe precisely the failure mode that occurs, and propose the minimum architectural fix.
Complete Solution
Part (a) — Asynchronous vs synchronous interrupts
A hardware interrupt is asynchronous: it can arrive at any point in the CPU clock cycle, completely independent of the instruction pipeline. The interrupt signal is generated by external hardware (a GPIO state change, a timer overflow, etc.) which has no knowledge of or relationship to the CPU's internal clock. A software interrupt (like a timer-triggered ISR scheduled by software) is synchronous — it fires at a predictable, deterministic point relative to the program flow, because software explicitly sets the condition. Hardware interrupts require the CPU to synchronise the external signal onto its clock domain (using flip-flops), whereas software interrupts are already in the clock domain.
Part (b) — RISC vs CISC interrupt latency
RISC (1-cycle instructions): Worst case — the interrupt arrives just as a new instruction begins. The CPU completes that instruction (1 cycle) then services the interrupt. Maximum additional latency: 1 clock cycle. At 80 MHz (ESP8266), that's 12.5 ns.

CISC (1–20 cycle instructions): Worst case — the interrupt arrives just as a 20-cycle instruction begins. The CPU must complete it before responding. Maximum additional latency: 20 clock cycles. The worst-case latency is 20× higher, and critically, it is non-deterministic — you can't know in advance how long the current instruction will take.

RISC is categorically better for real-time control because: (1) lower maximum latency, and (2) bounded and deterministic latency, which is essential for guaranteeing response time in control systems.
For real-time systems, determinism matters more than raw speed. A slow but predictable response is schedulable; a fast but unpredictable one is not.
Part (c) — ISR overrun failure mode and fix
The failure is interrupt overrun / deadline miss. The ISR takes 800μs; the motor loop must run every 1000μs. Each time the ISR fires, it consumes 800μs, leaving only 200μs for the main loop. If interrupts fire more than once per millisecond (or even once, depending on scheduling), the motor loop never gets its full execution time. Over multiple cycles, the motor loop may miss multiple iterations entirely, causing jerky or uncontrolled motor behaviour. In the worst case, a second interrupt fires while the ISR is running (if interrupts are not disabled), causing nested interrupts and stack overflow.

Minimum fix: The ISR should only set a flag variable (e.g., volatile bool sensorTriggered = true) and return immediately — execution time drops to microseconds. The heavy processing is deferred to the main loop, which checks the flag each cycle. This pattern is called "deferred processing" or "bottom half" handling.

A golden rule of embedded systems: ISRs must be fast. "Fast" means microseconds, not milliseconds. The ISR's job is to record that something happened — not to process it.

Q.09
PWM and the Illusion of Analog
PWM HARDWARE QUANTITATIVE
(a) PWM creates an "illusion" of analog output from a digital pin. Explain the physical mechanism by which a human eye perceives 50% duty cycle as medium brightness. What is the minimum frequency required, and why?
(b) Software PWM is "prone to glitches." Identify the root cause: what event can disrupt a software PWM signal that cannot disrupt hardware PWM?
(c) The ESP8266 runs at 80 MHz. A hardware PWM module uses a 16-bit prescaler to divide the clock. Calculate the minimum achievable PWM frequency and the maximum achievable PWM frequency. What determines the resolution (number of distinguishable brightness levels)?
Complete Solution
Part (a) — Physical mechanism of perceived brightness
The human eye's photoreceptors have a temporal integration time of approximately 50–100ms (corresponding to the ~20 Hz flicker fusion threshold). When an LED flashes faster than this, the visual system cannot distinguish individual on/off events and instead perceives the time-averaged intensity. A 50% duty cycle LED at sufficient frequency delivers half the photons per unit time as a continuously-on LED — perceived as half brightness. The minimum frequency for flicker-free perception is ~50–60 Hz (PAL/NTSC frame rates were chosen for this reason). In practice, 1 kHz+ is used for PWM to ensure no flicker under any viewing conditions (including peripheral vision, which is more sensitive to flicker).
Part (b) — Root cause of software PWM glitches
Software PWM is generated by the CPU toggling a GPIO pin in software at calculated time intervals. Any hardware interrupt that fires while the CPU is managing the PWM timing will delay the toggle. The CPU must pause the PWM timing loop, execute the ISR, then return. This creates an unpredictable jitter in the PWM period — the pin stays HIGH or LOW longer than intended, creating a glitch. Hardware PWM uses a dedicated hardware timer and comparator that operates completely independently of the CPU. Even if the CPU is fully occupied with an ISR, the hardware timer continues counting and toggles the pin at the exact programmed moment. No software path can disturb it.
Software PWM: any interrupt = potential glitch. Hardware PWM: CPU-independent, glitch-free by construction.
Part (c) — PWM frequency range and resolution
A 16-bit prescaler divides the 80 MHz clock by values from 1 to 65,536.

The PWM period = prescaler × (number of counter steps). Assuming a separate N-bit counter for duty cycle:

Maximum PWM frequency (prescaler = 1, minimum period): f_max = 80,000,000 / (1 × counter_period). For a 1-bit resolution counter (just on/off), f_max = 80 MHz / 2 = 40 MHz. For useful 8-bit resolution: f_max = 80,000,000 / 256 ≈ 312.5 kHz.

Minimum PWM frequency (prescaler = 65,536, 8-bit counter): f_min = 80,000,000 / (65,536 × 256) ≈ 4.77 Hz.

Resolution is determined by how many distinct duty cycle steps the counter provides: an 8-bit counter → 256 levels (0–255). A 10-bit counter → 1024 levels. Higher resolution requires a longer period (lower maximum frequency). There is a fundamental trade-off: resolution × frequency = constant for a given clock speed.

You cannot have both high frequency AND high resolution with a fixed clock speed. A 10-bit 80 MHz PWM tops out at 80MHz/1024 ≈ 78 kHz. To exceed this, you must increase the CPU clock — which is why high-end motor controllers run at 200+ MHz.

You are designing an embedded weather station that must: read temperature via I2C (address 0x48), read wind speed via SPI anemometer, transmit data via UART to a computer at 115200 baud, alert via LED PWM when temperature exceeds threshold, wake from deep sleep every 60 seconds, and run on battery (low power critical).

Part (b) — Why LOW to RST wakes the device
RST (Reset) is an active-low signal. It is held HIGH by a pull-up resistor during normal operation. When RST is pulled LOW, it triggers a reset of the CPU — the processor restarts from the beginning of its boot sequence, re-initializing all peripherals. During deep sleep, the CPU is powered down; GPIO16 is one of the few pins that remains active. When the internal timer expires, GPIO16 outputs a LOW pulse. This LOW on RST causes a hardware reset — which is the mechanism by which the chip "wakes up" (it actually restarts). The device perceives this not as waking from sleep but as a cold reset, and the user code checks a flag in RTC memory to know whether it's a fresh boot or a sleep-wake cycle.
Part (c) — Concurrent UART + I2C operation
Yes, simultaneous UART transmission and I2C conversion is possible without data corruption, for the following architectural reasons:

1. Physical independence: UART uses GPIO1/3; I2C uses GPIO4/5. They are entirely separate pins and separate hardware peripheral modules.
2. Independent hardware controllers: Both UART and I2C have dedicated hardware shift registers and state machines. Once a UART byte is loaded, it transmits autonomously. The I2C peripheral independently manages the clock and data lines.
3. No shared bus: Unlike two I2C devices that share SDA/SCL, UART and I2C share no signal lines.

The only concern is CPU interrupt load — if both peripherals generate interrupts simultaneously, interrupt latency for one may increase. Using hardware peripherals (not bit-banged software) mitigates this significantly.
Part (d) — Argument against Raspberry Pi for weather station
1. Power consumption: Raspberry Pi 5 draws ~5W under load; ESP8266 draws ~80mW active and ~20μA in deep sleep. For a battery-powered device, the Pi would drain a typical battery in hours; the ESP8266 can last months with deep sleep cycling.

2. Boot time: Raspberry Pi requires ~20–30 seconds to boot its Linux OS. The ESP8266 wakes from deep sleep in ~2ms. For a system waking every 60 seconds, the Pi would spend 30–50% of its awake time just booting.

3. No OS required / self-sufficient: This is a simple sensor-read + transmit task. A Raspberry Pi's full Linux OS, filesystem, and multi-process environment are all overhead that provides zero value for this workload. The ESP8266 is purpose-built for exactly this use case.

4. Reliability / failure modes: A Linux SD card can corrupt on unexpected power loss (exactly what happens during battery discharge). The ESP8266 has no moving storage — it boots from Flash on every reset, making it robust to power interruptions.

5. Cost and size: Raspberry Pi 5 costs ~$60+ and requires external power supply; ESP8266 NodeMCU costs ~$3 and runs directly from battery.

The right tool for the job: a Raspberry Pi is a pocket computer. An ESP8266 is a sensor node. Bringing a mainframe to read a thermometer is engineering malpractice.

BONUS The Aha Question
Q.11
The Bus Width Paradox
BONUS BUS WIDTH SYNTHESIS AHA MOMENT
A 32-bit microcontroller and an 8-bit microcontroller both need to perform the same computation tasks.
(a) The 32-bit MCU must load 8-bit values into 32-bit registers to add them. Has any computational advantage been gained for this specific operation? What has been wasted?
(b) Now the task changes: add two 32-bit numbers on the 8-bit MCU. How many operations are required? Show the step-by-step breakdown.
(c) In what real embedded application might an 8-bit microcontroller be the objectively superior choice over a 32-bit one, even when cost is equal? Your answer must invoke at least two technical properties beyond simply "it's cheaper."
Complete Solution
Part (a) — 8-bit operation on 32-bit MCU
For adding two 8-bit numbers, the 32-bit MCU gains no computational advantage. The result still fits in 8 bits (or at most 9 bits with carry). The computation completes in exactly 1 instruction cycle on both. What is wasted: (1) 24 of 32 register bits hold meaningless zeros, (2) the full 32-bit memory bus is used to transfer 8 bits of meaningful data — 75% bus bandwidth is wasted, (3) the instruction decoder, ALU, and register file consume full 32-bit operating power for 8-bit work. This is why low-power sensor nodes often use 8-bit MCUs even today — working with naturally 8-bit sensor data (temperatures, ADC readings) wastes nothing.
Part (b) — 32-bit addition on 8-bit MCU
The 8-bit MCU must decompose the 32-bit addition into 8-bit chunks, propagating the carry bit:

Step 1: ADD the lowest bytes (bits 0–7) of both numbers. Store result byte 0. Record carry.
Step 2: ADC (Add with Carry) the next bytes (bits 8–15). Store result byte 1. Record carry.
Step 3: ADC the next bytes (bits 16–23). Store result byte 2. Record carry.
Step 4: ADC the highest bytes (bits 24–31). Store result byte 3.

Total: 4 instructions (1 ADD + 3 ADC) plus additional LOAD/STORE instructions to move bytes from memory to registers. Realistically 10–12 instructions total for a full 32-bit add on an 8-bit MCU.
The carry flag is the bridge between bytes. Multi-precision arithmetic works by chaining 8-bit operations through the carry bit — the same principle used in grade school long addition.
Part (c) — When 8-bit is objectively superior
Application: Simple keyboard matrix scanner or basic appliance controller (e.g., washing machine button panel)

Technical superiority argument 1 — Power consumption: 8-bit MCUs operate with simpler internal logic and fewer transistors switching per cycle. At the same clock frequency, an 8-bit MCU dissipates dramatically less power. For a device running on two AA batteries for years (TV remote control, smoke alarm), an 8-bit MCU in sleep mode drawing 1μA versus a 32-bit MCU drawing 10μA represents a 10× battery life difference — objectively superior regardless of price.

Technical superiority argument 2 — Code simplicity and determinism: A 32-bit MCU with complex peripherals, DMA channels, multiple interrupt priorities, and cache introduces complex timing non-determinism. An 8-bit MCU with straightforward, simple peripherals is easier to reason about for safety-critical or time-critical simple loops. In industrial PLCs and automotive body control modules, 8-bit MCUs are still preferred precisely because their behaviour is completely predictable and certifiable.

The 8-bit MCU excels where data is naturally 8-bit, power is constrained, and simplicity/predictability is valued over raw computational power.

The ultimate insight: "better" in engineering always means "better for the constraints of this specific problem." A 32-bit MCU is not unconditionally superior to an 8-bit one — it is superior for computationally intensive tasks and inferior for ultra-low-power, simple, long-running applications. Every specification lives on a trade-off surface.
